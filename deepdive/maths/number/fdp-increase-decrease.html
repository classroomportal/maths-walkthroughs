Increase, decrease, and reverse percentages

Stage 1

Percentage Increase & Decrease — Add/Subtract Method

To increase or decrease an amount by a percentage: find that percentage of the amount (Part A, Stage 6), then add it on (increase) or take it away (decrease).

Worked example: Increase £160 by 35%.
35% of 160 = 0.35 × 160 = £56
New amount = 160 + 56 = £216
Decrease £160 by 35%.
35% of 160 = £56
New amount = 160 − 56 = £104
Stage 2

Percentage Increase & Decrease — Multiplier Method

There's a faster one-step way: build a single multiplier.

To increase by p%: multiplier = 1 + (p ÷ 100)
To decrease by p%: multiplier = 1 − (p ÷ 100)
Worked example: Increase £160 by 35%.
Multiplier = 1 + 0.35 = 1.35
1.35 × 160 = £216 — same answer as Stage 1, in one step.
160 + 0.35×160 is the same as 1×160 + 0.35×160 = (1+0.35)×160 = 1.35×160. Adding then is baked directly into a single multiplier.
Stage 3

Real-Life Contexts

These skills show up constantly in real situations: discounts (sale prices), tax added to a price, and things going up or down in value over time.

Discount example: A £45 jacket has 20% off. New price = 45 × 0.8 = £36 (multiplier for a 20% decrease is 0.8).
Tax example: A £250 laptop has 7.5% tax added. New price = 250 × 1.075 = £268.75 (multiplier for a 7.5% increase is 1.075).
Stage 4

Repeated Percentage Change

Sometimes a percentage change happens more than once — like a price rising by the same percentage two years in a row. A common mistake is to just double the percentage. That's wrong, because the second change applies to the new amount, not the original.

Common error: "Increase by 10% twice" is NOT the same as "increase by 20%."
Worked example: £100 increases by 10%, then increases by 10% again.
After year 1: 100 × 1.1 = £110
After year 2: 110 × 1.1 = £121 (not £120!)
The second 10% is 10% of £110 (£11), not 10% of the original £100 (£10). £110 + £11 = £121. Each change compounds on top of the last result.
Stage 5

Reverse Percentages — the Idea

So far, every question has given you the original amount and asked for the new amount. Reverse percentage questions do the opposite: they give you the new amount and ask what the original was.

The trap almost everyone falls into: a shop item now costs £splitVal after a 20% price rise. You might think "20% of £splitVal, then subtract it" — but that's wrong, because 20% of the new price is not the same as 20% of the original price. The percentage was always taken of the original amount, which you don't know yet.
The fix: the new amount is the original × multiplier. So to undo it:
original = new amount ÷ multiplier
A price rises 20% to £120.
✗ Wrong: 20% of 120 = 24, so original = 120 − 24 = £96
✓ Correct: multiplier = 1.2, so original = 120 ÷ 1.2 = £100
Check: £100 × 1.2 = £120 ✓ — the wrong method doesn't check out (£96 × 1.2 = £115.20, not £120).

Quick check (no scoring): before moving on, make sure you can explain in your own words why you can't just find 20% of the new amount. When you're confident, continue.

Stage 6

Reverse Percentages — Worked Examples

Reversing an increase: After a 25% increase, a price is £150. Find the original.
Multiplier = 1 + 0.25 = 1.25
Original = 150 ÷ 1.25 = £120
After a 25% decrease, a price is £90. Find the original.
Multiplier = 1 − 0.25 = 0.75
Original = 90 ÷ 0.75 = £120
