Stage 1

Foundations: Lines and Angles

Every circle theorem in this walkthrough eventually reduces to a handful of facts about lines and angles. Rather than just listing them, we'll build them up one from another — starting from a single starting idiom, exactly the way Euclid did.

The Starting Idiom: Angles Round a Point = 360°

Stand at a point O and spin all the way around until you're facing your original direction again. That's a full turn. We define a full turn to be 360° — this single idiom is where everything else in this walkthrough comes from.

a + b + c + d = 360° — however many angles are arranged all the way round a point with no gaps or overlaps, they always sum to 360°.

Derived: Angles on a Straight Line = 180°

A straight line through O has two opposite rays, L and R.
Shade the turn on one side of the line red.
Shade the turn on the other side green. Together, red and green make up the full 360° turn around O. Rotating 180° about O swaps L and R with each other — that's exactly what makes the line straight rather than bent — and that same rotation swaps red and green too. So they must be equal: each is 360° ÷ 2 = 180°. This is why we say "angles on a straight line sum to 180°" — any angle or set of angles that together fill just one side (all of red, or all of green, or a few angles adding up to fill one side) will always total 180°. QED

Derived: Vertically Opposite Angles Are Equal

Two straight lines cross at O, forming four angles going around: angle AOC, angle COB, angle BOD, angle DOA.
Look at just angle AOC on its own, shown in red.
Now angle COB, shown in gold. angle AOC + angle COB = 180° (angles on a straight line — line AB, just proved above).
Now angle BOD, shown in red like AOC — this is the one we're trying to compare it to. angle COB + angle BOD = 180° (angles on a straight line — line CD, the same fact applied to the other line).
Both equations equal 180°, and angle COB (gold) is the exact same wedge in both of them — it hasn't moved. So we can subtract it from each side: angle AOC + angle COB = angle COB + angle BOD becomes angle AOC = angle BOD.
Vertically opposite angles are equal (and the same argument, using the gold and red swapped, gives angle COB = angle DOA). Built entirely from the straight-line fact, nothing new assumed. QED

A New Idiom: Parallel Lines

Corresponding angles are equal when two lines are parallel. Unlike everything above, this can't be derived from the point/line/vertical-angle facts alone — it's a genuinely new assumption, specific to parallel lines. It's essentially Euclid's fifth postulate (the "parallel postulate"), historically the most debated of his five axioms. We take it as given from here on.

α = α′ — same position at each intersection (the "F" shape). Taken as given for parallel lines; everything else about parallel-line angles is built from this plus the two facts above.

Derived: Alternate and Co-Interior Angles

Same set-up as above. Corresponding angles α (top intersection) and α′ (bottom intersection) are equal — our new parallel-lines idiom.
The angle vertically opposite α, at the same (top) intersection — call it β — also equals α (vertically opposite angles, already proved).
β and α′ are the alternate ("Z-shape") pair — both between the parallel lines, on opposite sides of the transversal. Since β = α = α′: alternate angles are equal.
Now take γ, the angle adjacent to α along the upper parallel line: γ = 180° − α (angles on a straight line).
γ and α′ are the co-interior ("C-shape") pair — both between the parallel lines, same side of the transversal. γ + α′ = (180° − α) + α = 180°: co-interior angles sum to 180°. Both facts built from just the vertical-angle proof and the parallel-lines idiom — nothing new assumed. QED

Part 1 — the idioms so far

Putting It Together: Angle Sum of a Triangle = 180°

Draw triangle ABC. Draw a line through C parallel to AB.
Using the alternate-angles fact we just derived: the angle at C on the left equals angle A (transversal AC cutting the two parallel lines).
Same fact again: the angle at C on the right equals angle B (transversal BC).
The three angles at C sit on a straight line, so they sum to 180° (our second idiom). Since they equal A, C, and B — angle A + angle B + angle C = 180°. QED — this whole proof rests on just two things: the straight-line fact and the alternate-angles fact, both derived above.
Exterior angle rule follows immediately: since interior angles sum to 180°, and the exterior angle plus its adjacent interior angle sum to 180° (angles on a line), the exterior angle must equal the sum of the two non-adjacent interior angles.

Stage 2

Polygons

Proof: angle sum of a polygon

Take any polygon with n sides. Pick one vertex.
Draw diagonals from that vertex to every other non-adjacent vertex. This splits the polygon into (n − 2) triangles.
Each triangle's angles sum to 180° (proved in Stage 1).
The triangle angles together make up exactly the polygon's interior angles, so interior angle sum = (n − 2) × 180°. QED
  • Exterior angles of any convex polygon sum to 360° (one full turn walking around the shape)
  • Regular polygon: each interior angle = (n−2)×180° ÷ n; each exterior angle = 360° ÷ n
  • Special quadrilaterals (parallelogram, kite, trapezium) — their angle and side properties can all be proved from triangle congruence and parallel line facts, not just stated
Stage 3

The Bridge: Isosceles Triangles in Circles

This is the single most important idea in the whole topic. Two radii drawn to any two points on a circle always form an isosceles triangle, because all radii are equal in length. Every circle theorem in Stage 4 is really just this fact applied cleverly.

Centre O. Points A and B on the circumference. Draw radii OA and OB.
OA = OB (both are radii of the same circle).
Triangle OAB is isosceles, so the base angles are equal: angle OAB = angle OBA.
This one fact — base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal — is the engine behind every proof in Stage 4.

Practice before we name any circle theorem — this is pure isosceles-triangle reasoning.

Stage 4

Circle Theorems — Part A: From the Centre

Angle at the centre = twice the angle at the circumference

Centre O, points A, B, C on the circle. Draw OA, OB, OC, then extend CO to a point D outside the circle.
Triangle OAC is isosceles (OA = OC), so angle OAC = angle OCA. Exterior angle AOD = angle OAC + angle OCA = 2 × angle OCA.
Triangle OBC is isosceles (OB = OC), so similarly angle BOD = 2 × angle OCB.
Adding: angle AOB = angle AOD + angle BOD = 2(angle OCA + angle OCB) = 2 × angle ACB. So the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference, standing on the same arc AB. QED

Angle in a semicircle = 90°

AB is a diameter, so it passes through centre O. The angle at the centre, angle AOB, is a straight line — 180°.
By the theorem just proved, the angle at the circumference is half the angle at the centre.
So angle ACB = 180° ÷ 2 = 90°, for any point C on the circumference. QED

Part 1 — angle at the centre

Angles in the same segment are equal

Chord AB divides the circle into two arcs. Take two points C and D on the same arc (the same segment), both "looking at" chord AB.
Draw OA and OB. Angle AOB is the angle at the centre standing on arc AB.
By the centre theorem: angle ACB = ½ × angle AOB (C is on the circumference, same arc AB).
By the same theorem applied to D: angle ADB = ½ × angle AOB. Since both equal ½ × angle AOB, angle ACB = angle ADB. QED

Cyclic quadrilateral: opposite angles sum to 180°

ABCD is a cyclic quadrilateral, centre O. Draw radii OB and OD.
Angle BAD (vertex A) stands on arc BCD. By the centre theorem, the central angle for that arc — angle BOD measured going round through C — equals 2 × angle BAD.
Angle BCD (vertex C) stands on arc BAD. The central angle for THAT arc — angle BOD measured going round through A — equals 2 × angle BCD.
The two versions of angle BOD together make a full turn: 2×angle BAD + 2×angle BCD = 360°, so angle BAD + angle BCD = 180°. QED (uses "angles at a point sum to 360°" from Stage 1)

Stage 5

Circle Theorems — Part B: Tangents

Tangent is perpendicular to the radius

A tangent line touches the circle at exactly one point, P. Draw the radius OP.
Take any other point Q on the tangent line (Q ≠ P). Since the line only touches the circle at P, Q lies outside the circle — so OQ > OP.
This makes OP the shortest possible line from O to the tangent line. The shortest distance from a point to a line is always along the perpendicular to that line.
So OP must be perpendicular to the tangent: angle OPQ = 90°. QED

Two tangents from an external point are equal in length

From external point X, draw two tangents touching the circle at P and Q. Draw radii OP, OQ, and the line OX.
Angle OPX = angle OQX = 90° (tangent ⊥ radius, proved above).
OP = OQ (both radii of the same circle), and OX is common to both triangles OPX and OQX.
Triangles OPX and OQX are congruent (RHS: right angle, hypotenuse OX, side OP = OQ). So XP = XQ. QED

Alternate segment theorem

Tangent touches the circle at P. Chord PQ makes angle θ with the tangent. We compare θ to the angle in the alternate segment.
Draw the diameter through P to the opposite point R (through centre O). Tangent ⊥ radius, so the angle between the tangent and PR is 90°. That means angle RPQ = 90° − θ.
Draw RQ. Since PR is a diameter, angle PQR = 90° (angle in a semicircle, Stage 4).
In triangle PQR, angles sum to 180°: angle PRQ = 180° − 90° − (90° − θ) = θ. Angle PRQ is the angle in the alternate segment — so it equals θ, the tangent-chord angle. QED (uses angle-in-semicircle + tangent⊥radius + angle sum of a triangle)
Stage 6

Synthesis: Angle Chasing with Reasons

In exam-style and formal proof answers, every line needs a statement and a reason. This is what full marks look like:

Given: ABCD is a cyclic quadrilateral, angle DAB = 100°. Find angle BCD.
Statement: angle DAB + angle BCD = 180°  Reason: opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral sum to 180°.
Statement: angle BCD = 180° − 100° = 80°  Reason: rearranging the equation above.
Notice: the reason always cites a named fact (isosceles triangle, parallel lines, angle sum, or a proved circle theorem) — never "it looks like it."
