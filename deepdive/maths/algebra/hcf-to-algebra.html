Deep Dive: Highest Common Factor

From factors of numbers all the way to factorising algebraic expressions

Before You Start

Stage 1

What is a Highest Common Factor?

Key words
Factor — a number that divides exactly into another number, with nothing left over.
Common — shared by two or more things.
Highest — the biggest one.

A factor of a number is a whole number that divides into it with nothing left over.

Another way to think about it: if two numbers multiply together to make 12 (like 3 × 4 = 12), then both 3 and 4 are factors of 12.

Factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12
(12 ÷ 3 = 4 exactly, so 3 is a factor. 12 ÷ 5 = 2.4, so 5 is NOT a factor.)
Factors of 18: 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18

Where the two circles overlap (cross over), those numbers are factors of both 12 and 18 — they are common factors.

Common factors: 1, 2, 3, 6  →  the highest (biggest) one is 6.
Highest Common Factor (HCF) of 12 and 18 = 6
Stage 2

Prime Factorisation — Two Ways to See It

Listing every factor gets slow for bigger numbers. Instead, we can break a number down into prime factors — numbers that can't be split any further (like 2, 3, 5, 7...).

There are two common paper methods for this. They always give the same answer — just drawn differently.

Split 36 into two factors, then keep splitting each branch until every end is a prime number (can't split any further).

Divide by the smallest prime number that goes in exactly, write the answer underneath, and repeat until you reach 1.

Tree and ladder both end up with the same set of primes: 36 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3
Use whichever picture makes more sense to you — they always match.
Stage 3

Finding HCF using Prime Factorisation

Once two numbers are broken into prime factors, their HCF is simply the primes they share.

36 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3    48 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 3

Shared primes: 2 × 2 × 3 = HCF = 12
Stage 4

HCF of Algebraic Terms

The same tree idea from Stage 2 works for terms with letters too. Remember: x² means x × x, and y³ means y × y × y — so a term like 8x²y can be split into branches exactly like a number can.

Each term splits into its number branch (broken down just like Stage 2) plus one branch per letter.

8x²y

12xy³

Reading the leaves of each tree gives the same breakdown, just written in a row instead of branches:

8x²y = 2 × 2 × 2 × x × x × y

12xy³ = 2 × 2 × 3 × x × y × y × y

The gold pieces below appear in both breakdowns — those are the pieces the HCF is built from:

Shared pieces: 2 × 2 × x × y  →  HCF of 8x²y and 12xy³ = 4xy
Stage 5

Using HCF to Factorise Expressions

Factorising means writing an expression as the HCF multiplied by whatever's left over in brackets.

HCF of 6x² and 9x is 3x
6x² ÷ 3x = 2x    9x ÷ 3x = 3
6x² + 9x = 3x(2x + 3)
Stage 6

Factorising Quadratic Trinomials

Stage 5 worked because 6x² and 9x shared a common factor. But x² + 5x + 6 has no common factor at all (the HCF of x², 5x, and 6 is just 1) — so it needs two brackets instead of one.

Key rule — for x² + bx + c, find two numbers that multiply to give c and add to give b.

We need two numbers that multiply to 6 and add to 5. Check the factor pairs of 6:

1 × 6 = 6, but 1 + 6 = 7 ✗
2 × 3 = 6, and 2 + 3 = 5 ✓
x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3)

The same rule works with negatives — just make sure the sign of each number is right.

x² − x − 6: need two numbers that multiply to −6 and add to −1.
2 × (−3) = −6, and 2 + (−3) = −1 ✓
x² − x − 6 = (x + 2)(x − 3)
