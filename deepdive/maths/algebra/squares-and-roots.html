Deep Dive: Quadratics — Part A

Squares & Square Roots — the foundation for surds and quadratic equations later in this chain

Before You Start

How long will this take?
Most students: around 45–55 minutes.
Still building confidence? Take up to 70 minutes, over more than one sitting if needed — your progress is saved as you go.
Already confident? You might move through it in 25–30 minutes.
Stage 1

What Does "Squaring" Mean?

Key words
Square (a number) — multiply a number by itself.
Square number — the answer you get when you square a whole number.

To square a number means to multiply it by itself. We write "4 squared" as 4².

4² means 4 × 4 — not 4 × 2. This is a common slip, so watch out for it.

If you make a square shape with 4 little squares along each side, you get 16 little squares in total — that's where the name comes from.

4² = 4 × 4 = 16
1² = 1    2² = 4    3² = 9    4² = 16    5² = 25
6² = 36    7² = 49    8² = 64    9² = 81    10² = 100
These are called perfect squares — worth memorising up to at least 12² = 144.
Stage 2

Square Roots — Undoing a Square

A square root asks the opposite question: "what number, multiplied by itself, gives this answer?" The symbol is √.

We already know 4 × 4 = 16 from Stage 1. So the question "what squares to make 16?" has the answer 4.

√16 = 4    (because 4² = 16)
Squaring and square-rooting are opposite (inverse) operations — just like + and − undo each other, or × and ÷ undo each other.
4² = 16     √16 = 4
Stage 3

Estimating Square Roots

Not every number is a perfect square. √20 doesn't come out as a whole number — but we can still estimate it sensibly.

Find the perfect squares just below and above 20: 16 (=4²) and 25 (=5²). So √20 must be somewhere between 4 and 5.

20 is closer to 16 than to 25, so √20 is a bit closer to 4 than to 5 — a sensible estimate is about 4.4 or 4.5.
(The exact value is 4.472... — your estimate doesn't need to be exact, just sensible.)
Stage 4

Every Positive Number Has TWO Square Roots

Remember that multiplying two negative numbers gives a positive answer. This matters for square roots.

(−4)² = (−4) × (−4) = 16

So −4, just like +4, squares to give 16.

The two square roots of 16 are +4 and −4, written √16 = ±4.
This "two answers" idea will matter a lot later, when solving quadratic equations — they often have two solutions for exactly this reason.
Stage 5

Rational and Irrational Numbers

Key words
Rational number — can be written as a fraction (one whole number divided by another).
Irrational number — cannot be written as a fraction; its decimal goes on forever without ever repeating.

Square roots of perfect squares are always whole numbers — so they're rational. But what about square roots of numbers that aren't perfect squares, like √20 from Stage 3?

√16 = 4, and 4 can be written as the fraction 4/1. So √16 is rational.
√20 = 4.472135955... — the decimal carries on forever, with no repeating pattern. It can't be written as an exact fraction. So √20 is irrational.
The square root of a perfect square is always rational (a whole number).
The square root of any other whole number is always irrational.
Stage 6

Surds — Simplifying

Key words
Surd — a square root left in root form, like √3, because it's irrational.

To simplify a surd, pull out any perfect square factor.

√12 = √4 × √3 = 2√3
3 has no perfect square factor left, so this is fully simplified.
Stage 7

Surds — Adding & Subtracting

Key words
Like surds — surds with the same number under the root, like 2√3 and 5√3.

You can only add or subtract like surds — same idea as "3 apples + 2 apples", not "3 apples + 2 oranges".

2√3 + 5√3 = 7√3
Add the numbers in front, keep the surd the same.
2√3 + 5√2  →  cannot be simplified
Different surds — just leave it as it is.
Stage 8

Surds — Multiplying

√a × √b = √(a × b)
√3 × √5 = √15
√2 × √8 = √16 = 4
Always check if the result can be simplified afterwards.
Stage 9

Surds — Rationalising the Denominator

Key words
Rationalise — rewrite a fraction so there's no surd on the bottom.

Dividing by a surd is awkward. Fix: multiply top and bottom by the same surd.

1√2 = 1 × √2√2 × √2 = √22
√2 × √2 = 2 — the surd on the bottom disappears.
